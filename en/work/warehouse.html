Keeping scans uninterruptedWarehouse Inbound & Outbound System

A warehouse system that pairs barcode printers and scanners to replace manual work and Excel. After launch, I went back to the floor and found that staff never looked at the screen while scanning — so I redesigned how focus moves between fields to keep their scanning rhythm unbroken.

Role
Sole UI/UX Designer
Team
1 PM, 1 UI/UX, 2 Engineers
Users
Purchasing staff, warehouse staff
Timeline
Feb – May 2025 (3 months)

The Problem

Warehouse work used to rely on manual processes and Excel. The PM brought the request, and I talked with warehouse staff about how they actually worked, which surfaced three problems.

Design & Iteration

Version 1 | Replacing Excel

Barcode scanning instead of manual entry

Version 1 paired barcode printers and scanners so staff could scan instead of typing into Excel, eliminating typos from manual entry. But after scanning an item, they still had to click the location field before scanning the location barcode.

Version 2 | Post-launch iteration

Letting the cursor follow the workflow

Version 1 replaced Excel, but when I observed on site, I found that staff barely looked up at the screen while scanning. If the cursor sat in the wrong field, they had to switch it by hand — and once the rhythm broke, input errors and missed scans went up noticeably.

Design Rationale

The system was digital, but the workflow still had to fit how people worked on the floor, so I improved three areas:

  • Workflow: Focus changes follow the rhythm of holding a scanner and checking goods by eye, cutting down on switching between hands, eyes and screen.
  • Real-time feedback: Different sounds and colors distinguish three states — a normal scan, all units received (ready to scan the location), and an off-list item — so staff know where they are without looking at the screen.
  • Scanning rules: Items can have multiple units and be scanned in any order, so I worked through the logic with engineers to balance floor efficiency with technical feasibility.

Logic Rules

  1. Receiving dialog opensFocus starts in the item barcode field — no click needed
  2. Item barcode scannedThe matching item’s received count goes up by 1 and progress updates instantly
  3. Items scanned in any orderAs long as they’re on the same receiving list, switching from item A to item B mid-way adds to B without affecting A’s count
  4. Item not on the receiving listThe field turns red and a message appears, with a repeating alert sound unlike the success sound; the count doesn’t change
  5. Received = expected (100%)A distinct completion sound signals it’s time to scan the location; the cursor jumps to that item’s location field and shows “Scan the location”
  6. Location barcode scannedThe location fills in and the cursor returns to the item barcode field
  7. All items at 100% with locations filledOnly then is the “Complete” button enabled

Design Process

  1. ResearchUnderstanding the context

    After the PM raised the need, I asked warehouse staff how they actually worked and identified too many steps, repeated counts and out-of-sync data.

  2. DesignVersion 1

    Paired barcode printers and scanners so scanning replaced typing, eliminating input errors and giving real-time inventory visibility.

  3. After launchBack on the floor

    Saw that staff didn’t look at the screen while scanning, and a cursor in the wrong field broke their rhythm.

  4. IterationRedesigning focus

    After each scan, the cursor jumps to the next field automatically, so continuous scanning isn’t interrupted.

  2. 2Purchasing & management

  3. 3Finance

Reflection

This project taught me that launching a system doesn’t mean the design is done. Looking back, two lessons stood out:

  • The real problems only show up on site. Version 1 had already replaced Excel, but it wasn’t until I watched staff at work that I saw they barely looked at the screen while scanning. Even after digitizing, whether a workflow fits the floor still has to be tested in context.
  • Feedback should follow the user’s attention. Their eyes were on the goods, so I used sound and color to signal scan status and reduce how often they had to look up.

A good system doesn’t just digitize a process — it lets people focus on the work in their hands.
